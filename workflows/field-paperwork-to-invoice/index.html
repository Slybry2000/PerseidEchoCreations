Field paperwork

The job is done. Now somebody retypes it.

The waste in a field-service business is almost never in the field. It is in the trip the paperwork makes afterwards, and that trip is fixable.

Run the 60-second Fit Check Buy or build?

What actually happens

Nobody decided to let this happen.

Work happens at a site. Hours end up in a text message, materials on a paper ticket, photos on somebody's phone, and the note about what actually changed on the job in somebody's head. Later — usually much later, usually by whoever is still there — all of that gets reassembled into a job record and an invoice.

Every step of that reassembly is a chance to lose an hour, a detail, or a billable item. The billing goes out late, it goes out light, and the person doing it is normally the most expensive person available.

Work out your own number

The second number is usually the one that surprises people. Under-billing from reconstructed records tends to be invisible, because nobody ever sees the invoice that should have been larger.

The honest version

Buy a tool, or build it?

Buy the tool if

  • Your work is uniform enough that one job form fits nearly all of it.
  • You are willing to move your scheduling and invoicing into the same product to get the integration.
  • Your crews will adopt a new app, and you are confident about that rather than hopeful.
  • You do not need the job record to reach anything outside that product.

Build it if

  • You already run scheduling in one system and accounts in another, and neither is moving.
  • Field-service platforms have been tried here before and did not stick.
  • What has to be captured varies enough by job type that a fixed form either asks too much or too little.
  • The office side, not the field side, is where the real time goes — the reassembly, not the recording.

The all-in-one field-service platforms are genuinely good and if you fit their shape you should buy one. The reason to build is almost always that you have existing systems you are not willing to abandon, and that is a legitimate reason, not stubbornness.

If it is the second one

What a build actually has to do

Part 1

Captured once, where it happens

The hard rule: the person in the field does less than they did with the paper ticket, not more. If the capture step is heavier than what it replaces, it will not get used, and the build has failed regardless of how good the office side is.

Part 2

The office side assembles itself

Hours, materials, photos and notes land against the right job without anybody rekeying them. This is usually where most of the recovered time actually is.

Part 3

Exceptions get raised, not buried

A job that finished without hours logged, or with a photo and no note, gets flagged before it becomes an invoice. Catching the gap the same day is worth more than any amount of chasing later.

When to leave this alone

Do not build this yet if…

If you run one or two crews and the paperwork already lands the same day, this is not your first build. The pattern is worth fixing when the reassembly has become somebody's evening job.

The part most people worry about

Nothing gets authority by accident.

Every workflow I build has a setting for how much it is allowed to do on its own, and you pick it per workflow rather than once for the whole business.

Setting one

It drafts, you send

The system does the assembling and the remembering. A person reads it and presses the button. Where most work should start, and where a lot of it should stay.

Setting two

It runs, you get told

It acts on the rules you agreed and reports what it did. Suitable once a workflow has proven itself and the cost of a wrong move is small.

Setting three

It runs, you can stop it

Fully hands-off with a switch you own and a record of every action. Appropriate for high-volume, low-judgement work, and not much else.

Straight answers

Questions this one always raises

My crews will not use a complicated app.

Then it is the wrong build, and I will say so rather than ship it. The bar is that the field step is lighter than the paper ticket it replaces. If I cannot get under that bar with your work, the honest answer is to fix the office side only and leave the field alone.

Can this work with the accounting system we already have?

Usually, and connecting to it is normally cheaper than replacing it. That is the main argument for building rather than buying here: you keep the systems your business already runs on and close the gap between them, instead of migrating everything to get one feature.
