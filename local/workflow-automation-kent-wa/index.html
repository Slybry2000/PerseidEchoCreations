What the work looks like here

Kent is not a generic market, and this is not a generic page.

Kent has one of the densest concentrations of warehousing, distribution and light manufacturing on the West Coast. That produces a very particular kind of repetitive work: order intake off email and PDFs, purchase orders matched against invoices, inventory counts reconciled between a spreadsheet and whatever the system says, driver and dispatch paperwork chased down after the fact.

It is high-volume, low-judgement work, and it is almost always being done by somebody who is too senior to be doing it. That combination — frequent, tedious, and expensive when it goes wrong — is the clearest case for building something. It is also the case where I am most likely to tell you the fix is a better process rather than a bigger tool.

Where I would look first

Three jobs worth checking in a Kent business

These are the patterns I see most often in this part of the county. Not a promise that yours is one of them — that is what the Fit Check and the Map are for.

Candidate 1

Order intake off email and PDFs

Orders arrive as attachments and somebody retypes them into the system. High frequency, real cost when a digit moves. Usually the first thing I look at in a Kent Valley business.

Candidate 2

PO and invoice matching

Three documents that should agree, checked by eye. This is where the money quietly leaks, and it is well-suited to a system that flags the exceptions and leaves the judgement calls to a person.

Candidate 3

Field and driver paperwork

Proof of delivery, hours, damage notes — captured on paper or in a phone, then chased at the end of the week. Getting it captured once, at the point it happens, is often the whole fix.

The part most people worry about

Nothing gets authority by accident.

Every workflow I build has a setting for how much it is allowed to do on its own, and you pick it per workflow rather than once for the whole business.

Setting one

It drafts, you send

The system does the assembling and the remembering. A person reads it and presses the button. Where most work should start, and where a lot of it should stay.

Setting two

It runs, you get told

It acts on the rules you agreed and reports what it did. Suitable once a workflow has proven itself and the cost of a wrong move is small.

Setting three

It runs, you can stop it

Fully hands-off with a switch you own and a record of every action. Appropriate for high-volume, low-judgement work, and not much else.

Straight answers

Questions Kent owners actually ask

We already have an ERP or a WMS. Does that make this pointless?

Usually the opposite. The expensive work is almost never inside the system of record, it is in the gaps around it — the email that has to become a record, the spreadsheet that reconciles two systems, the report somebody rebuilds every Monday. I would rather connect what you already pay for than sell you something new.

What if the answer is that we should not automate this?

Then that is the answer, and you get it for free in about a minute. The Fit Check genuinely returns “do not automate this yet” when the work is infrequent or a miss costs nothing, and I have not tuned that away to win more business. A build on the wrong workflow is worse than no build: you pay for it, then you maintain it, then you work around it.

Are you going to replace someone's job?

That is not what this work is for and it is not usually where the money is. The jobs worth building are the ones nobody's title covers — the retyping, the chasing, the reconciling that gets done at seven in the evening by whoever is still there. Taking that off a person's plate is the point.

How long before something is actually working?

A Map takes about a week from the call. A first build is usually two to four weeks after that, depending on how many systems have to agree and how quickly I can get access to them. I would rather ship one workflow that works than four that half-work.
