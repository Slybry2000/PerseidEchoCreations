What the work looks like here

Auburn is not a generic market, and this is not a generic page.

Auburn carries the south end of the Kent Valley industrial belt plus a strong base of trades and field-service businesses — contractors, equipment and repair outfits, landscaping, transport. The defining feature is that the work happens away from the office, and the record of it has to travel.

So the same pattern shows up again and again: a job is done, then someone reconstructs it later from photos, texts, a paper ticket and memory, and turns that into a schedule update, an invoice and a follow-up. Each step is a chance to lose an hour or a detail. Capturing it once, where it happens, is normally the whole build.

Three jobs worth checking in a Auburn business

Candidate 1

Field capture to office record

Hours, photos, materials and notes taken once at the job and landing in the right place without a second pass. The highest-value fix in most field businesses.

Candidate 2

Quoting and the chase after it

Getting the quote out faster matters less than making sure it gets followed up. A system that never forgets is worth more here than a system that writes prettier quotes.

Candidate 3

Scheduling around the day falling apart

Routes and crews are set, then a job overruns. The build worth doing is the one that handles the reshuffle and tells the customer, not the one that draws the original plan.

The part most people worry about

Nothing gets authority by accident.

Every workflow I build has a setting for how much it is allowed to do on its own, and you pick it per workflow rather than once for the whole business.

Setting one

It drafts, you send

The system does the assembling and the remembering. A person reads it and presses the button. Where most work should start, and where a lot of it should stay.

Setting two

It runs, you get told

It acts on the rules you agreed and reports what it did. Suitable once a workflow has proven itself and the cost of a wrong move is small.

Setting three

It runs, you can stop it

Fully hands-off with a switch you own and a record of every action. Appropriate for high-volume, low-judgement work, and not much else.

Straight answers

Questions Auburn owners actually ask

My crews are not going to use a complicated app.

Then it is the wrong build. If the capture step asks a crew to do more work than the paper ticket did, it will not get used and I will have wasted your money. The bar I hold is that the person in the field does less, not more. If I cannot get under that bar for your work, I will say so.

What if the answer is that we should not automate this?

Then that is the answer, and you get it for free in about a minute. The Fit Check genuinely returns “do not automate this yet” when the work is infrequent or a miss costs nothing, and I have not tuned that away to win more business. A build on the wrong workflow is worse than no build: you pay for it, then you maintain it, then you work around it.

Are you going to replace someone's job?

That is not what this work is for and it is not usually where the money is. The jobs worth building are the ones nobody's title covers — the retyping, the chasing, the reconciling that gets done at seven in the evening by whoever is still there. Taking that off a person's plate is the point.

How long before something is actually working?

A Map takes about a week from the call. A first build is usually two to four weeks after that, depending on how many systems have to agree and how quickly I can get access to them. I would rather ship one workflow that works than four that half-work.
