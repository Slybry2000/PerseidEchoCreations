What the work looks like here

Renton is not a generic market, and this is not a generic page.

Renton is not one economy, it is three sitting on top of each other. There is the Boeing supply chain and the machine shops and fabricators that feed it. There is a deep bench of owner-led trades — electrical, HVAC, auto, landscaping, remodels — a lot of them running two vans and a whiteboard. And there is the Highlands and downtown service layer: clinics, dental practices, restaurants, studios.

What those three have in common is that the back office never got built. The work got bigger, the paperwork got bigger with it, and nobody ever stopped to design the part where information moves from one place to another. That gap is where I do most of my work.

Where I would look first

Three jobs worth checking in a Renton business

These are the patterns I see most often in this part of the county. Not a promise that yours is one of them — that is what the Fit Check and the Map are for.

Candidate 1

Quotes that go out and never come back

The single most common one I find in Renton trades. The quote gets sent, the owner means to follow up, the week happens. A system that chases every quote on a schedule you set is usually the cheapest thing on the list and the fastest to pay for itself.

Candidate 2

The job-to-invoice handoff

Work gets done in the field, then somebody retypes it in the office at night. Photos in one place, hours in another, the invoice built from memory. This is normally a plumbing problem, not an AI problem, and it fixes cleanly.

Candidate 3

Intake for practices and clinics

New patient or new client turns into a stack of forms somebody keys in by hand, and the same details get asked for three times. Worth mapping when it happens often enough, and worth leaving alone if it does not.

The part most people worry about

Nothing gets authority by accident.

Every workflow I build has a setting for how much it is allowed to do on its own, and you pick it per workflow rather than once for the whole business.

Setting one

It drafts, you send

The system does the assembling and the remembering. A person reads it and presses the button. Where most work should start, and where a lot of it should stay.

Setting two

It runs, you get told

It acts on the rules you agreed and reports what it did. Suitable once a workflow has proven itself and the cost of a wrong move is small.

Setting three

It runs, you can stop it

Fully hands-off with a switch you own and a record of every action. Appropriate for high-volume, low-judgement work, and not much else.

Questions Renton owners actually ask

Do you actually work with businesses in Renton, or is this a page for the search engine?

I am in Renton. That is where the business is registered and where I work from, so a first conversation here can be in person if you would rather do it that way. I have deliberately not written pages for cities I could not get to.

What if the answer is that we should not automate this?

Then that is the answer, and you get it for free in about a minute. The Fit Check genuinely returns “do not automate this yet” when the work is infrequent or a miss costs nothing, and I have not tuned that away to win more business. A build on the wrong workflow is worse than no build: you pay for it, then you maintain it, then you work around it.

Are you going to replace someone's job?

That is not what this work is for and it is not usually where the money is. The jobs worth building are the ones nobody's title covers — the retyping, the chasing, the reconciling that gets done at seven in the evening by whoever is still there. Taking that off a person's plate is the point.

How long before something is actually working?

A Map takes about a week from the call. A first build is usually two to four weeks after that, depending on how many systems have to agree and how quickly I can get access to them. I would rather ship one workflow that works than four that half-work.
