The gap

The advice is right. The system never gets built.

You have probably already told a client some version of the list below. Knowing it should happen is rarely the hard part, and hearing it again next quarter is not what changes anything.

  • Follow up with every lead, every time
  • Write down the process that lives in one person’s head
  • Stop retyping the same details into three different tools
  • Say the same thing to every customer, not whichever version somebody remembers
  • Get the weekly numbers without losing a Friday afternoon to them
  • Take the repetitive administrative work off skilled people

The hard part is getting it built, connected, adopted, and kept running after you have moved on to the next piece of work. That is the implementation gap I fill.

Ways to work together

Three ways to work together.

Which one fits depends on how involved you want to be, and how much of the client relationship you want to keep carrying yourself. You can use different ones for different clients.

  • Way 01

    Refer

    You make the introduction. I scope, contract, build, and support the system directly with the client. You stay informed at whatever level the two of you want.

    • Best when you have spotted a strong opportunity and would rather not carry a technical project.
  • Way 02

    Collaborate

    You stay in the discovery, the process design, and the adoption work. I take the technical review, the build, the testing, and the support afterwards.

    • Best when the system supports a larger coaching or consulting engagement you are already running.
  • Way 03

    Embed

    Implementation delivered inside your own client offer. Branding, client communication, pricing, and who answers the phone are all settled in writing before anything is sold.

    • Offered once we have delivered a client engagement together and each of us knows how the other works.

Who handles what

The line is drawn on purpose.

Most joint work goes wrong because nobody wrote down who owns which half. So this is written down, and it is the same on every engagement.

The technical half, start to finish.

  • Workflow mapping and technical feasibility review
  • Solution design, including what should stay human
  • The automation and integration build
  • Testing, and the approval controls around sensitive actions
  • Documentation the client keeps and controls
  • Training the people who will use it
  • Monitoring, maintenance, and tool-change adjustments
  • Exception handling, where the client has bought it
  • Technical support after launch

What you handle

The half you were already doing, at whatever depth the engagement calls for.

  • Spotting the business problem worth solving
  • The strategic context behind it
  • Supporting the process decisions
  • Preparing the client for the change
  • Reinforcing adoption once it is live
  • The wider coaching or consulting relationship

You never have to scope technical requirements or say what a system will be able to do. I review the workflow first, and the read comes back to you before it goes anywhere near your client.

Partner engagement standard

Form PS-01 · Applies to every introduction

  1. Nothing is promised before a technical review.

    No quote, no scope, no commitment until I have looked at the real workflow and the tools it runs on. Neither of us gets to guess in front of your client.

  2. The scope goes in writing.

    What the system does, what it deliberately does not do, what it costs to build, what it costs to run, and who is responsible for each part of it.

  3. The client always knows who supports what.

    No wondering whether a question goes to you or to me. Your client gets one clear answer about who to call, and it is written down before launch.

  4. Sensitive actions sit behind an approval.

    Customer-facing and high-impact actions receive the control level agreed with the client: draft only, approval required, or automatic within defined rules. Anything unusual stops instead of guessing.

  5. Everything is tested and documented before handoff.

    Your client gets documentation they own and training on the system they now depend on, so the knowledge does not live only with me.

  6. Problems get surfaced, not hidden.

    When a vendor changes something underneath a system, or a workflow outgrows the way it was built, you hear it from me first and you hear it early enough to do something with it.

  7. Access stays inside the project.

    Client data and system access cover what the automation actually needs, and the connection ends cleanly whenever the client says so.

The point is not to impress your client with AI. The point is to make the process you recommended work the way you said it would.

Qualifying an opportunity

What kind of client problem fits.

The strong ones look alike. The work repeats often, it follows rules you could write down, it moves between more than one tool, and it is the first thing to slip when the business gets busy. It eats skilled time without needing skilled judgement.

  • Fit 01

    Lead and quote follow-up

    Inquiries logged, answered quickly, and worked on a cadence instead of whenever somebody remembers.

  • Fit 02

    Booking and reminders

    Confirmations and reminders that cut no-shows without a person spending their morning chasing them.

  • Fit 03

    Quotes, invoices, and job paperwork

    Documents assembled from details already sitting in their systems, held for a person to approve.

  • Fit 04

    Reactivation and reviews

    Past customers contacted again, and review requests sent on a schedule that actually holds.

  • Fit 05

    Internal reporting

    The weekly numbers assembled on their own, sitting there before the meeting instead of after it.

  • Fit 06

    Moving data between systems

    The same details ending their trip through three tools by hand, with one system agreed as the source of truth.

Not every process should be automated, and part of my job is telling you plainly which parts should stay human. You can pass that answer to your client with your name on it.

How an opportunity moves

From your conversation to a working system.

Five steps, and you can stop after any of them. Nothing reaches your client until you have seen it first.

  1. 01

    You bring the problem

    What the client is trying to improve, what happens today, and where it breaks down. A paragraph is enough to start. You do not need to know what the solution looks like.

  2. 02

    I review the workflow

    What is feasible, what should stay human, which tools are involved, and what needs controlling. You get that read before your client does, so nothing lands in front of them that you have not already seen.

  3. 03

    We pick the delivery model

    Refer, collaborate, or embed, based on how involved you want to be and what the client actually needs. Compensation is settled here, in writing, not after the fact.

  4. 04

    The client gets a written scope

    The outcome, who is responsible for what, the build cost, the running costs, and the support arrangement. They sign knowing exactly what they are getting and who to call.

  5. 05

    I build it and remain accountable through launch

    I handle the implementation, testing, documentation and training. After launch, my responsibility follows the maintenance or managed-service arrangement the client chooses. You stay involved at the level we agreed and nowhere beyond it.
